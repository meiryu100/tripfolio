Americans travel by car more than any other way: for the 2025 Thanksgiving period alone, AAA projected a record 81.8 million travelers, about 73.3 million of them on the road ([Washington Times, Nov 2025](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2025/nov/17/aaa-predicts-record-high-818-million-people-travel-thanksgiving/)). All those drives add up, which raises a simple question: how many of the 50 states have you actually been to?

Want to skip ahead? Color them in on the [US states visited map](/us-states-visited-map). It works without signing up and counts them out of 50 as you tap.

Here's how to count honestly, settle the arguments, and end up with a map you can keep.

## Start with the road trips, not the flights

Most people undercount. Flights blur together, but road trips leave a trail: the state-line signs, the gas station in the middle of nowhere, the motel with the questionable carpet.

Go through your life in three passes:

- **Where you grew up and where you've lived.** That's usually two to four states before you've even left home.
- **Family trips and holidays.** Grandparents, cousins, the lake house, the one reunion in a state you'd never otherwise visit.
- **Big road trips.** Cross-country drives pick up a lot of states in a hurry, including ones you only crossed on the way somewhere else. (Whether crossing counts is the next section.)

Your camera roll is the best memory aid you have. Search by year, scroll, and write down every state you see. Photos of a sunset in an unfamiliar parking lot are surprisingly good evidence.

## The rules: what counts as "visited"?

There is no official ruling, so decide on your own rules and keep them consistent. These are the usual lines people draw:

- **Layover.** If you never left the airport, most travelers say it doesn't count. If you cleared the terminal and had a meal in town, it does.
- **Driving through.** A common rule is that you need to have stopped: fuel, food, a night's sleep, a short walk. Passing through at 70 mph with the cruise control on is a gray area.
- **Overnight.** The strictest rule, and the cleanest: you slept there. Some people use this for countries, too.
- **Kids and babies.** Taken as a baby and remembered by nobody? Most people count it anyway. It's your map.

One more detail that trips people up: Washington, D.C. is not a state. It's a federal district, so it's not part of the 50. Tripfolio's counter works the same way: it counts the 50 states and leaves D.C. out of the "x / 50" number.

## Make the map

## Plan the next ten

The best part of a states map is the orange and the gray: the gaps. Some ideas for filling them in:

- **The neighbors.** Look at the gray states touching your green ones. Those are the easiest weekend trips.
- **The corners.** Maine, Washington, Florida and southern California or Texas make a satisfying frame for a map, and each is a trip in itself.
- **A theme.** National parks, baseball stadiums, the Pacific coast, the places in your favorite movie. A theme turns a list into a plan.
- **Friends.** Ask a friend who's been somewhere what to do there. On Tripfolio you can follow friends and see which countries they've been to or want to visit, which is a faster way to get recommendations than scrolling through old posts.

## What's a good number?

There's no good or bad number. People who grew up in a small state next to several others can reach 10 quickly; people who've lived in a big one might have a long list and still be missing a dozen. The point isn't to win. The point is to see your life on a map and realize which part of the country you've been ignoring.

## FAQ

### How many US states have I visited?

List the states you've lived in, then family trips, then road trips, and check your camera roll for the ones you forgot. Then mark them on a states map, which counts them out of 50 for you.

### Does driving through a state count as visiting?

There's no official rule. A common one is that you need to have stopped, for fuel, food or a night's sleep. Passing straight through is a gray area, so pick a rule and keep it consistent.

### Does Washington, D.C. count as a state?

No. Washington, D.C. is a federal district, not a state, so it isn't part of the 50. Puerto Rico, Guam and the other territories aren't states either.

### What's the average number of states people have visited?

There's no reliable official figure, and surveys vary a lot. What matters more is your own map: the gray states next to your green ones are usually the easiest next trips.
