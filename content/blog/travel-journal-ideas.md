The best travel journal ideas are small and specific: write three lines a day, list what you ate, note one sentence someone said to you, or keep a running list of tiny details like prices, smells and sounds. You don't need to write well or every day. You need a format light enough that you'll still use it on day nine, when you're tired and the train is late.

Below are 25 prompts, five formats you can pick from, and a simple routine for before, during and after a trip.

## Why keep a travel journal at all?

Photos show what a place looked like. A journal keeps what it was like: the argument about the bus ticket, the woman who gave you directions in three languages, the taste of the soup you can no longer name. Those details fade faster than you expect, and a camera roll doesn't hold them.

Writing about travel is also an old habit. A travel journal is a record made by a traveler, sometimes in diary form, and many travelers throughout history kept diaries with no plan to publish them. Some became books: James Boswell's *The Journal of a Tour to the Hebrides* appeared in 1786, and Goethe's *Italian Journey* was drawn from diaries of his 1786–1788 trip ([Wikipedia: Travel literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travel_literature)). Yours doesn't have to be a book. It only has to be yours.

## How do you start a travel journal if you hate writing?

Lower the bar until it's almost silly:

- **Three lines a day.** One about the place, one about a person, one about how you felt. That's it.
- **Write at the same time every day.** On the bus, over the first coffee or in bed. A fixed moment beats willpower.
- **Don't edit.** A first draft is the point. Spelling, grammar and neat handwriting don't matter.
- **Use whatever you carry.** A paper notebook, the notes app on your phone or voice memos you transcribe later. The best journal is the one that is with you.
- **Skip days without guilt.** A gap is not a failure. Write "two days of nothing but trains" and move on.

## What are some easy travel journal formats?

You don't have to write paragraphs. Pick one format and stick with it for the trip.

### 1. The three-line daily entry

Place, person, feeling. Fast and easy to keep up for weeks.

### 2. The list journal

Only lists: things you ate, words you learned, prices you paid, the contents of your bag, places you wanted to come back to. Lists are quick to write and surprisingly vivid to reread.

### 3. The postcard to yourself

Write each entry as a short postcard, addressed to you, with a greeting and a sign-off. The small frame makes it easier to start.

### 4. The sketch and caption

If you like to draw, a quick sketch of a street or a plate with a single caption says plenty. Bad drawings are fine, and they often make better memories than good ones.

### 5. The photo-plus-sentence

Take your best photo of the day and write one sentence about what is not in it: the noise, the heat, what happened just before. This format pairs the camera with the words.

## What are good travel journal prompts?

Keep a short list in the back of your notebook and pick one when you're stuck.

### Before the trip

1. Why am I going to this place now, and not last year?
2. What do I expect it to be like, and what am I afraid of?
3. What is the one thing I really don't want to miss?
4. Who would I like to bring along, and what would I tell them about it?

### During the trip

5. What was the first thing I noticed when I arrived?
6. What did I eat today, and who made it?
7. What sounds and smells would I never find at home?
8. Which words did I learn today, and what do they mean?
9. What did someone say to me that I want to remember?
10. What surprised me today?
11. What did I get wrong today, and what did it teach me?
12. What is the most ordinary thing I saw that struck me as strange?
13. If I could only keep one photo from today, which would it be, and what's outside the frame?
14. What would I do differently if I did today again?
15. What am I grateful for right now?
16. What does the light look like at this time of day here?

### After the trip

17. Which day would I live again, and why?
18. What did I pack and never use? What did I wish I had?
19. Which person from this trip do I want to thank or write to?
20. What did this place change about how I think of home?
21. What would I tell a friend who is going next month?
22. What would I skip next time, and what would I stay longer for?
23. How much did the trip really cost, and was it worth it?
24. Which three photos tell the story of this trip?
25. Where do I want to go next, and what did this trip teach me about picking it?

## What should you write down while you're there?

If prompts feel too abstract, collect raw material and shape it later. These small facts are the ones people regret not noting:

- **Dates and the order of places.** You'll forget whether Kyoto came before Osaka. A simple list of where you slept each night solves it.
- **Names.** The guesthouse owner, the guide, the person who shared a table.
- **Prices.** What you paid for a meal, a ticket, a taxi. Great for planning your next visit, and good for a laugh later.
- **Exact words.** A phrase you heard, a sign you misread, a joke that only works in context.
- **Sensory details.** Temperature, noise, smell, the feel of a place at 7 a.m.

Your phone helps with some of this without extra effort. Digital cameras and phones record the date and time of each photo in its metadata, and many with built-in GPS also store the location ([Wikipedia: Exif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exif)). That means your photos can quietly reconstruct the timeline, and the journal can focus on what the photos can't hold.

## How do you turn a travel journal into something you'll reread?

Notes you never open aren't memories, they're clutter. A few habits help:

- **Do a ten-minute review at the end of the trip.** Reread everything, underline the lines you love and add what you now remember.
- **Write a one-paragraph summary.** What happened, what mattered, what you'd tell a friend.
- **Pick a headline for each trip.** "The week it rained in Lisbon" is easier to find again than "Portugal 2026".
- **Put it with the photos.** A story sitting next to its pictures gets reread. A notebook in a drawer rarely does.
- **Revisit it once a year.** Pick a date, like the anniversary of a trip, and reread an old one.

## FAQ

### What should I write in a travel journal?

Write what a photo can't show: the people you met, what you ate, how a place sounded and smelled, what surprised you and how you felt. Add the basics too, like dates, names and prices, so the story has a skeleton.

### How often should I write in a travel journal?

Daily is ideal, but a few lines every day or two is enough. Choose a fixed time, such as the evening or a long journey, and don't worry about missed days.

### How do I start a travel journal?

Start with one page: where you are, what you did and one thing you noticed. Pick a simple format, like three lines a day or a list, and keep the first entry short so that writing the second feels easy.

### Is a digital or a paper travel journal better?

Neither is better. Paper feels personal and works without a battery, while digital notes are searchable and easy to back up and attach photos to. Choose the one you'll actually keep up, or mix both.
