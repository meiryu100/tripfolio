A dependent territory is a place that is not fully independent, but is also not an ordinary part of the country that controls it. [Wikipedia's definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dependent_territory) is that it does not possess full political independence or sovereignty as a sovereign state, and it stays politically outside the controlling state's integral area. Puerto Rico, Bermuda and Guam are dependent territories. Hong Kong and Greenland are close cousins that sit in a gray zone.

If you count countries, this matters, because it decides whether a trip to the Cayman Islands or Greenland goes on your "countries" number or somewhere else. This guide explains what a dependent territory is, the main types, how it differs from a country, and a simple way to count them.

## What is a dependent territory, in plain words?

Think of three levels:

1. **A sovereign state** is fully independent. It makes its own laws, runs its own foreign policy and has no other state above it. Examples: Portugal, Japan, Kenya.
2. **A normal part of a state** is a region, province or department. It has the same status as the rest of the country, even if it is far away. France's overseas departments such as Réunion or French Guiana are like this: Wikipedia notes they have the same status as the regions of mainland France.
3. **A dependent territory** sits in between. It has its own government and often its own passport rules, currency or tax system, but a sovereign state is ultimately responsible for defense and foreign affairs.

Wikipedia adds that dependent territories often have more autonomy than a normal administrative division. But autonomy isn't universal: not all autonomous places are dependent territories, and not all dependent territories are autonomous.

You'll also see the words *dependency*, *overseas territory*, *external territory* and *crown dependency*. They are labels each country uses for its own arrangement, so they don't line up perfectly.

## What are the main types of dependent territory?

The labels depend on who is in charge. Wikipedia's article lists these examples by sovereign state:

- **United Kingdom:** 3 Crown Dependencies (Guernsey, Jersey and the Isle of Man) and 13 Overseas Territories, such as Bermuda, Gibraltar and the Cayman Islands.
- **United States:** 13 unincorporated territories, five of them inhabited: Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands.
- **Australia:** 6 external territories, three of them inhabited: Christmas Island, the Cocos (Keeling) Islands and Norfolk Island.
- **New Zealand:** Tokelau, plus two self-governing states in free association, the Cook Islands and Niue.
- **Norway:** Bouvet Island, plus Svalbard, which Wikipedia lists as a similar entity rather than a dependent territory.
- **France:** overseas collectivities such as French Polynesia, and New Caledonia, which has a special status of its own.

Some arrangements look like dependent territories but are described differently. Wikipedia calls Aruba, Curaçao and Sint Maarten constituent countries of the Netherlands, and the Faroe Islands and Greenland autonomous territories of Denmark. They are officially integral parts of their states, but they have so much self-government that travelers treat them as separate places.

## How is a dependent territory different from a country?

Four differences come up most often:

- **Sovereignty.** A country is sovereign. A dependent territory is not.
- **Membership in the UN.** The [United Nations has 193 member states](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/member-states). Dependent territories are not members.
- **Defense and foreign policy.** These usually belong to the controlling state.
- **Everyday life.** Here the picture flips. Many territories have their own flag, license plates, tourism board, stamps in your passport and sports teams. They feel like countries when you visit.

That last point is why the question is so confusing for travelers. A place can feel completely different from its controlling state, yet legally still belong to it.

## How many dependent territories are there?

There is no single number, because lists disagree on what to include. Does a place count if it has no residents? What about a claim in Antarctica? Is a constituent country a dependent territory?

The most official list is the United Nations' list of Non-Self-Governing Territories. Under Chapter XI of the UN Charter, these are places "whose people have not yet attained a full measure of self-government." According to [Wikipedia's summary of the list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_list_of_non-self-governing_territories), it currently has 17 entries, including American Samoa, Bermuda, the Cayman Islands, Gibraltar, Guam, French Polynesia, New Caledonia and Western Sahara. That list is about decolonization, so it leaves out many places travelers think of as territories, such as Puerto Rico, Greenland or Hong Kong.

So the honest answer is that the number depends on the list you pick. In the other direction, the Travelers' Century Club counts 330 countries and territories, a figure we cover in our guide on [what counts as visiting a country](/blog/what-counts-as-visiting-a-country).

## Is Puerto Rico a country? Is Hong Kong?

Neither is a sovereign country, and both are good examples of how different the arrangements are.

- **[Puerto Rico](/countries/puerto-rico)** is a U.S. unincorporated territory. Its residents hold U.S. citizenship, but the island is not one of the 50 states.
- **[Hong Kong](/countries/hong-kong)** and **[Macau](/countries/macau)** are special administrative regions of China. They have their own border controls and currency, which is why they feel like separate destinations.
- **[Greenland](/countries/greenland)** has been self-ruling within the Kingdom of Denmark since 2009. It is huge, but it is not a sovereign state.
- **[Bermuda](/countries/bermuda)** is a British Overseas Territory and is on the UN's list of Non-Self-Governing Territories.

None of them is a UN member or a sovereign state. All of them are real places with their own character, and many travelers want to count them somewhere.

## Do dependent territories count as countries visited?

It depends on the standard you choose. There are two sensible ones, and our [guide to how many countries there are in the world](/blog/how-many-countries-are-there-in-the-world) explains the first.

- **Strict count: 195.** The 193 UN member states, plus the two permanent observer states, the Holy See and Palestine. This is the most common standard for "how many countries have you visited?" Territories don't count here.
- **Extended count.** The 195 plus the territories you've actually visited, written as a separate number: "34 countries and 6 territories."

Most people who keep both numbers find that it ends the arguments. Nobody can say you inflated your count, and the Cayman Islands still get their moment.

A few places are worth knowing because lists treat them differently: the Cook Islands and Niue are self-governing in free association with New Zealand, and Tripfolio files them as territories, not among the 195.

## How does Tripfolio handle dependent territories?

Tripfolio's world map has about 250 entries. The **195 sovereign countries** are what count toward your "x / 195" total, your percentage of the world and your milestones at 1, 10, 25, 50 and 100.

Territories are on the map too. You can mark [Puerto Rico](/countries/puerto-rico), [Guam](/countries/guam), [Aruba](/countries/aruba) or [French Guiana](/countries/french-guiana) as *Visited* or *Want to visit*, and they are colored like any other place. They just aren't counted in the 195. That matches the strict-plus-extended approach above: your headline number stays clean, and your territories stay on the map.

Some entries are filed in ways you might not expect: the Faroe Islands and Greenland, for instance, are listed as non-sovereign. Our guide to [how many countries are in the Caribbean](/blog/how-many-countries-in-the-caribbean) shows how many of that region's entries are territories.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between a dependent territory and a colony?

A colony is the historical term, and most colonies were dependent territories. Today the word *colony* is rarely used officially, and territories are described by the arrangement each country uses, such as overseas territory or external territory.

### Is a dependent territory a country?

No. A dependent territory is not a sovereign state and is not a UN member. It can still have its own government, flag and rules for visitors, which is why it can feel like a country.

### Is Puerto Rico a dependent territory?

Puerto Rico is a U.S. unincorporated territory, one of 13 that Wikipedia lists for the United States. It is not a state and not a sovereign country.

### How many territories are on the UN list of Non-Self-Governing Territories?

Wikipedia's page on the list shows 17 territories today, including Bermuda, Guam, Gibraltar and Western Sahara. The list covers decolonization, so it is shorter than most travelers' lists of territories.

### Do territories count toward 195 countries?

No. The 195 is the 193 UN member states plus the Holy See and Palestine. Territories are usually counted as a separate number.
