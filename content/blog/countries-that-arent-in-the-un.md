Every widely recognized country is in the United Nations except two: the **Holy See (Vatican City)** and the **State of Palestine**, which are non-member observer states. Beyond those two, a handful of places that govern themselves, such as Taiwan, Kosovo, the Cook Islands and Niue, aren't UN members either, and whether to call them "countries" depends on who you ask.

This guide lists the countries that aren't in the UN, explains why each one is outside, and shows how to count them honestly on a travel list without picking a political fight.

## Which countries aren't members of the UN?

The UN has [193 member states](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/member-states). Everything else falls into one of three groups:

1. **Observer states (2).** The Holy See and Palestine.
2. **Self-governing places with disputed or limited recognition.** Taiwan, Kosovo, Western Sahara, Somaliland, Northern Cyprus, Abkhazia, South Ossetia and Transnistria.
3. **Self-governing places in free association with another country.** The Cook Islands and Niue, both linked to New Zealand.

Dependent territories such as Greenland, Puerto Rico or Hong Kong are a different case again. They belong to another country, so nobody expects them to have a UN seat.

## What is an observer state?

The UN's page on [non-member states](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/non-member-states) defines these as places that have received a standing invitation to take part in General Assembly sessions and work as observers, and that keep a Permanent Observer Mission at UN headquarters. There are two:

- **The Holy See**, which represents [Vatican City](/countries/vatican-city), the smallest country in the world.
- **The State of Palestine**, which the General Assembly gave non-member observer state status in resolution A/RES/67/19 on 29 November 2012. See [Palestine](/countries/palestine).

An observer can speak and take part in debates but has no vote. Add the two to the 193 members and you get **195**, the number most travelers and atlases use. We explain the other totals people quote, 206, 211 and 330, in [how many countries there are in the world](/blog/how-many-countries-are-there-in-the-world).

## Why aren't Taiwan and Kosovo in the UN?

Because UN membership needs a recommendation from the Security Council and a vote in the General Assembly, and both places have powerful countries on the other side of the argument. Neither is a question of whether you can travel there. You can.

### Taiwan

Taiwan has its own government, currency, army and passport. The Republic of China held China's UN seat until 1971, when it passed to the People's Republic of China. Today, according to Wikipedia's [list of states with limited recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_states_with_limited_recognition), Taiwan has formal diplomatic recognition from 11 UN members and the Holy See. Many more countries trade and keep unofficial offices there without recognizing it formally. Read more about [Taiwan](/countries/taiwan).

### Kosovo

Kosovo declared independence from Serbia in 2008. The same list says 110 UN members recognize it (and Taiwan, the Cook Islands and Niue do too), while eight others have withdrawn recognition. Serbia and several other countries don't recognize it, and Kosovo hasn't won a UN seat. See [Kosovo](/countries/kosovo).

Figures like these change as countries add or withdraw recognition, so treat them as a snapshot from the date you read them.

## What about the Cook Islands and Niue?

These two are the odd ones out, because nobody disputes that they run their own affairs. The [Cook Islands](/countries/cook-islands) is a self-governing state in free association with New Zealand. Its constitution dates from 4 August 1965, and New Zealand cannot pass laws for the islands. Niue has a similar arrangement from 1974.

Neither is a UN member, but both belong to several UN specialized agencies, and the UN Secretariat has recognized their treaty-making capacity. According to Wikipedia, the Cook Islands' government has said it doesn't meet the requirements for UN membership, and joining would involve a referendum and rethinking the relationship with New Zealand. Cook Islanders can also hold New Zealand citizenship. See [Niue](/countries/niue) too.

## Which other places have limited recognition?

Some more places run themselves but have little or no international recognition. Wikipedia's list gives these snapshots:

- **Western Sahara** is claimed by Morocco, and the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic, which controls part of the territory, is recognized by 46 UN members, with others having withdrawn or suspended recognition. The African Union counts it as a member. See [Western Sahara](/countries/western-sahara).
- **Somaliland** declared independence from Somalia in 1991. Only one UN member (Israel) recognizes it.
- **Northern Cyprus** has been recognized by only one UN member, Turkey, since 1983.
- **Abkhazia and South Ossetia** broke away from Georgia and are recognized by a few countries, including Russia.
- **Transnistria** is recognized by no UN member; the only recognition it has comes from Abkhazia and South Ossetia.

These places raise strong feelings, so a fair approach for a travel list is to say which source you follow and leave it there. We try to do the same.

## Do you count these places as visited?

That's your call, as long as you pick a rule and keep to it. There are three sensible ways to handle it:

1. **UN-based count (195).** The 193 members plus the two observers. Simple, widely understood, and the one that avoids arguments.
2. **Count everything you personally consider a distinct destination.** Fine for a private list, but you should call it your own rule if you post it.
3. **Two tallies.** Keep the 195 as the headline, then a second number for the extra places, like "195 countries plus 4 others".

The third option is popular with collectors, and it respects that a trip to Taipei or Pristina feels like a trip to a different country, even when diplomats disagree. Our guide on [what counts as visiting a country](/blog/what-counts-as-visiting-a-country) covers the other half of the question: what you have to do in a place for it to count.

Some of the places above are on the map but marked as territories rather than counted: Taiwan, Kosovo, Western Sahara, the Cook Islands and Niue can all be colored as visited or as wanted, and none of them adds to the 195. ## FAQ

### What countries are not in the United Nations?

Two observer states, the Holy See (Vatican City) and Palestine, are outside as non-members. Taiwan, Kosovo, the Cook Islands, Niue, Western Sahara and several other self-governing places with limited recognition are also not members.

### Is Taiwan a UN member?

No. The Republic of China held China's seat until 1971, when the People's Republic of China took it over. Taiwan has formal diplomatic ties with 11 UN members and the Holy See, according to Wikipedia's list of states with limited recognition.

### Is Vatican City in the UN?

Not as a member. The Holy See, which represents Vatican City, is a non-member observer state with a standing invitation to the General Assembly.

### Is Kosovo a country?

Kosovo declared independence in 2008 and is recognized by 110 UN members according to Wikipedia, but not by Serbia and a number of other countries. It isn't a UN member.

### Are the Cook Islands in the UN?

No. The Cook Islands is a self-governing state in free association with New Zealand. It belongs to several UN agencies, such as the WHO and UNESCO, but not to the UN itself.
